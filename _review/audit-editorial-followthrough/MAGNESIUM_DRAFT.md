# Magnesium: read the amount, then ask what was tested

NON-PUBLIC DRAFT — September 10, 2026. Prepared after the K2 review/revision and zinc/copper draft, in that order. Clinical, company/compliance and publication review remain pending. No marketed product's dose, efficacy or suitability has been verified by this guide.

## Reader manuscript

Magnesium participates in normal nerve and muscle function and in making protein and bone. Foods such as beans, nuts, seeds, whole grains and leafy greens provide it. Those roles explain why magnesium matters; they do not show that an extra supplement will improve a particular symptom. [NIH consumer magnesium guide](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-Consumer/)

### The amount and the form answer different questions

On a Supplement Facts panel, the magnesium amount refers to elemental magnesium, not the weight of the whole magnesium compound. Check the serving size before comparing labels. [NIH professional magnesium guide](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/)

For example, a hypothetical serving of two capsules containing 200 mg of elemental magnesium supplies 100 mg per capsule. A large compound-weight number elsewhere on the package is not another elemental dose to add to that total. This arithmetic example is not a product label or an intake recommendation.

Names such as citrate, oxide and bisglycinate describe forms. Bisglycinate combines magnesium with glycine. A form name alone does not establish a health benefit. [Bisglycinate primary study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40918053/)

### Absorption does not settle every health question

Trials comparing forms often measure magnesium in urine or blood. One study in healthy adults found differences between citrate, an amino-acid chelate and oxide on absorption-related measures. It did not test whether one form made people sleep better or recover faster. Neither a favorable absorption measure nor an unfamiliar form name makes a product universally best. [Primary form-comparison study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14596323/)

There is some direct sleep research: a 2025 placebo-controlled bisglycinate trial reported a small improvement on a self-reported insomnia scale after four weeks. It did not establish objectively measured sleep improvement or long-term effectiveness. The result deserves its limited place in the evidence; it cannot support a promise that magnesium glycinate reliably fixes sleep for everyone. [2025 primary trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40918053/)

Evidence for one outcome cannot be recycled for another. A sleep study is not proof of anxiety relief, exercise recovery or fewer cramps, and a study of one preparation cannot validate every supplement with magnesium in its name.

### Intake references include different sources

NIH lists adult recommended total daily intakes of 400–420 mg for men and 310–320 mg for women, varying by age. These amounts include food; pregnancy has separate values. [NIH consumer intake table](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-Consumer/)

The adult upper limit of 350 mg/day applies to magnesium from supplements and medicines, excluding magnesium naturally present in food and beverages. This is why the upper-limit number can be lower than a total-intake recommendation. It is not a target dose. [NIH professional safety guidance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/)

### Include kidneys and medicines in the conversation

Supplemental magnesium can cause diarrhea, nausea and cramps. Kidney impairment increases toxicity risk. Magnesium also affects absorption of some antibiotics and oral bisphosphonates; some diuretics and long-term acid-suppressing medicines can alter magnesium status. Ask a pharmacist or prescriber about the exact medicine and timing, and include antacids and laxatives when listing products. [NIH professional safety and interaction guidance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/)

Bring the label and a clear question about the outcome you want. Persistent symptoms need an assessment of their cause; they are not a self-test for magnesium deficiency. This guide is education, not a diagnosis or personalized treatment plan.
