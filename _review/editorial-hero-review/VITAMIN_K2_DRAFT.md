# Vitamin K2: the form, the evidence and the questions to ask

**NON-PUBLIC EDITORIAL DRAFT - NOT APPROVED FOR PUBLICATION**

Prepared September 6, 2026. First guide in the K2 -> zinc/copper -> magnesium review sequence. No Library record, public route, search entry, campaign link or product association has been added.

## Reader draft

### Start with the name

Vitamin K is a family of nutrients. K1 is phylloquinone; K2 refers to menaquinones, including MK-4 and MK-7. These names describe different forms, not interchangeable evidence for every supplement carrying a K2 label. Vitamin K participates in normal blood clotting and bone-related protein processes. That biological role is not, by itself, proof that taking a particular supplement prevents disease. [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-HealthProfessional/)

### What can a study actually tell you?

Ask which form was tested, in whom, at what dose, for how long, and against what comparison. A change in a laboratory marker is a different outcome from fewer fractures or cardiovascular events.

For example, a randomized trial of 365 men with existing aortic valve calcification compared MK-7 plus vitamin D with placebo for two years. It did **not** find a significant reduction in the progression of valve calcification. The intervention combined two nutrients, and the participants had a specific condition; this does not settle every question about K2 or establish what another product will do. [Diederichsen and colleagues, Circulation, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35465686/)

### Read the label before reading the promise

Look for the named form, amount per serving and number of capsules in that serving. If D3 and K2 appear together, record each separately. If a form or amount is missing, ask for the current label; do not fill the gap from another brand's product or a study. A combination label alone does not demonstrate an added benefit.

### Medication context matters

Vitamin K can interact seriously with warfarin and similar vitamin K-antagonist anticoagulants. Consistent intake matters. If you take one of these medicines, discuss any supplement or dietary change with your prescriber or pharmacist first; do not change the medicine yourself. [NIH medication guidance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-HealthProfessional/)

**Bottom line:** distinguish nutrient biology, a study's actual outcome, and a specific product's evidence. This guide is educational, not a diagnosis, individual treatment plan or recommendation to buy a supplement.

## Editorial evidence notes - not reader copy

- **NIH ODS:** Health Professional Vitamin K fact sheet, accessed September 6, 2026; page reports update March 29, 2021. Supports terminology, nutrient-function context and medication interaction warning. Do not describe that publication as a 2026 update. It does not validate a marketed combination product.
- **Primary trial:** Diederichsen et al., PMID **35465686**, DOI **10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.121.057008**. Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 365 men; MK-7 720 micrograms plus vitamin D 25 micrograms daily for 24 months; primary outcome aortic-valve calcification progression, P=0.64. This is an evidence-literacy example, not dosing advice, a universal negative claim, or a current comprehensive evidence synthesis. The accessible PubMed abstract was checked; PMC full-text access presented a browser-check barrier, which was not bypassed. The full paper, supplements and disclosures still require review.
